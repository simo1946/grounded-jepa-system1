# 1. Introduction

A world model learns how the world changes when an agent acts. JEPA-style world models do this in a learned latent space: an encoder maps each camera frame to a compact vector, and a predictor learns how actions move that vector [1, 2]. The usual way to act with such a model is to plan. A planner such as the cross-entropy method (CEM) samples many action sequences, rolls each one forward in the latent, and executes the sequence whose imagined end state is closest to the goal image's latent. Planning is slow, and it works only if two things hold: the latent must contain the state the task is scored on, and the predictor must be fine enough to tell nearby actions apart. A small model may have neither.

This study started from a question in biology: whether a fast, direct decision step, which we call System 1 after the fast mode of thinking in dual-process accounts of cognition, could make automated experiments faster and more reliable. Robotic control is the most direct place to test such a decision step, so we test it there first; the robotics results do not by themselves establish anything about biological systems.

We take a small LeWM-style world model [1] on the Cube task of OGBench [3], a robot arm that must move a cube to a goal pose, and ask what limits goal-conditioned control when the model is small: what the latent represents, how the controller is structured, or how actions are chosen. We change one component at a time and compare with paired statistics over three training seeds. In this setting, how actions are searched for is not the main lever. A fast policy that maps the current latent and the goal latent directly to actions does better than search, and grounding the latent on the object the task is scored on matters most. Our contributions are:

- **A complementary evaluation for LeWM's Cube protocol.** OGBench scores Cube on its own evaluation tasks [3]; LeWM's evaluation code instead takes a start frame from the expert data and sets the goal to the frame {{set: 25 | goal offset in steps}} steps later [1]. # 2. Related work

**JEPA world models and latent planning.** LeWM trains an encoder and a predictor end to end from pixels, with a prediction loss and the SIGReg regulariser from LeJEPA [4], and plans with CEM in the latent [1]. DINO-WM plans over frozen DINOv2 features [2]. H-JEPA learns hierarchical JEPA world models end to end for visual planning [5]; we use its grasp-centred Cube windows as a second evaluation protocol. We use LeWM's code, data collector and evaluation through the stable-worldmodel library [6], and change what sits around the latent.

**Grounding the latent.** Inverse dynamics is used as an auxiliary loss for JEPA world models; in SMWM it is the only term that prevents representation collapse [7]. Our state-prediction head adds a different target: the robot's own state and, for object grounding, the object's position. It is used only in training. The novelty is the controlled measurement of what this changes for control, not the head itself.

**Amortised and hierarchical control.** System 1 is goal-conditioned behaviour cloning with hindsight relabelling, as in GCSL [8]. The subgoal network resembles HIQL [9]. We compare a Gaussian action head against an expressive flow-matching head, the family that includes Diffusion Policy [10] and rectified flow [11].

**Model-based verification and practice.** Verifying a policy's proposal with a model before acting, and residual reinforcement learning on top of a frozen policy (ResFiT [12], RFS [13]), motivate our selection and practice experiments. Potential-based reward shaping follows Ng et al. [14]. DAgger [15] is our corrective-label baseline. Our contrastive verifier follows the bidirectional InfoNCE objective of CLM [16], as used to verify robot actions in CoVer-VLA [17].

**Recent and concurrent work.** This study was developed independently of, and in parallel with, several recent studies on the same benchmarks that reach compatible conclusions by different routes. On grounding, PSG-JEPA grounds the latent on robot proprioception and joint-angle changes with training-only heads [19]; Liu et al. add inverse dynamics and state alignment to an end-to-end JEPA world model [20]; PhyLatent adds physical-state grounding and a counterfactual action-separation loss [21], related to our action-resolution loss. On control, GC-IDM amortises LeWM's planning into a goal-conditioned inverse-dynamics policy that beats CEM at a fraction of the cost [22]. It differs from our System 1 in ways that likely matter for absolute scores: it acts one step at a time from a fresh observation where System 1 commits to {{set: 25 | steps per System 1 chunk}} steps, it is told the remaining horizon, and it uses the frozen full-scale LeWM encoder, evaluated on the standard protocol (Section 5.8 tests the first two differences on our latents); LeFlow replaces CEM with flow-based latent planning [23]; LeWAM learns an action head jointly with the world model and finds that refining its proposals through the model helps on Push-T [24], whereas selection did not help in our Cube setting; Terver et al. study which design choices drive planning success with JEPA world models [25]; SpecWM replaces LeWM's anti-collapse regulariser with a loss that aligns the latent geometry with a target similarity kernel (temporal, quasimetric or state-based), raising CEM success on Cube from {{ext: 52% | [26] | CEM success on Cube with the LeWM loss in that paper}} to {{ext: 68% | [26] | CEM success on Cube with spectral alignment in that paper}} [26], a different route to the control-relevant representation that our grounding heads target. At the other end of the scale, RoboJEPA trains action-conditioned JEPA predictors of up to {{ext: 8 | [27] | largest predictor size in billions of parameters}} billion parameters on about {{ext: 15,000 | [27] | hours of robot video, rounded}} hours of robot video and plans with CEM [27]; it asks how world models scale, not how a small model is best used for control. What this study adds is a controlled, per-seed comparison of these ingredients in one setting: the object-position target against proprioception-only grounding, the non-trivial-pair evaluation, a privileged-ceiling diagnosis of why selection fails, and the hierarchy and memory results. Most of these works report Cube success on LeWM's standard protocol, on which most pairs are solved at the start ({{int: gpu_cube.standard_protocol.solved_at_start}} of {{int: gpu_cube.standard_protocol.pairs}} in our draw), which should be kept in mind when comparing numbers.

**State-space predictors.** Our hybrid predictor interleaves Mamba-3 layers [18] with attention, so that it can update the latent one step at a time like a filter.

# 3. Method

The system is LeWM's world model with training-only grounding heads, plus a fast policy that acts in its latent. The world model provides training signals, subgoals and state estimates; it does not choose every action (Figure 1).

**Units of time.** One *step* is one robot action (hand displacement, rotation and gripper command). One *block* is {{set: 5 | steps per action block}} steps; the world model predicts one latent per block. System 1 outputs a *chunk* of {{set: 5 | blocks per System 1 chunk}} blocks ({{set: 25 | steps per System 1 chunk}} steps). By default the robot executes the whole chunk and then observes and replans, so it decides every {{set: 25 | steps per System 1 chunk}} steps; in the camera-dropout and memory experiments it decides every block ({{set: 5 | steps per action block}} steps), and Section 5.8 also tests deciding after every step.

**World model.** A ViT-tiny encoder (patch size {{set: 8 | patch size}} on {{set: 64 | frame size in pixels}}-pixel frames) maps each frame to a {{set: 192 | latent width}}-dimensional latent. A {{set: 6 | predictor layers}}-layer transformer predictor takes the last {{set: 3 | latents of history}} latents and an embedded block of {{set: 5 | steps per action block}} actions and predicts the next latent. The loss is the mean squared error of the predicted latent plus {{set: 0.09 | SIGReg weight}} × SIGReg, as in LeWM [1]. Each model has about {{set: 18 | approximate world-model size in millions of parameters (Table 9 gives the exact counts)}} M parameters (Table 9).

**Grounding heads (training only).** An inverse-dynamics head predicts the action block from two consecutive latents (weight {{set: 1 | grounding head loss weight}}). A state-alignment head predicts the next state and its change from consecutive latents (weight {{set: 1 | grounding head loss weight}}). The state is the robot's proprioception (configuration C2) or proprioception plus the cube's position (C3, "object grounding"). The heads are discarded after training, so at test time the model sees pixels only. Inverse dynamics follows SMWM [7]; the state-alignment idea follows [20], with our own loss.

**Hybrid predictor (C4, C5).** The transformer is replaced by {{set: 6 | predictor layers}} layers in a {{set: 3 | Mamba-3 layers per attention layer}}:{{set: 1 | attention layers per group}} pattern of Mamba-3 and attention layers, with a mixture-of-experts feed-forward block ({{set: 8 | routed experts}} routed experts, top-{{set: 2 | experts per token}} routing, {{set: 1 | shared experts}} shared expert). The recurrent layers let the model update the latent one step at a time.

**System 1.** A goal-conditioned policy, a {{set: 3 | System 1 layers}}-layer MLP of {{num2: model_sizes.policies.system1 / 1000000}} M parameters, maps the current latent, the goal latent and their difference to a Gaussian over the next {{set: 5 | blocks per System 1 chunk}} blocks × {{set: 5 | steps per action block}} steps of actions. It is trained on cached latents with hindsight goals {{set: 1 | nearest hindsight goal in blocks}} to {{set: 5 | farthest hindsight goal in blocks}} blocks ahead: for each training window, a later frame of the same episode is used as the goal and the actions actually taken are the target. At test time it executes the mean chunk and replans after the whole chunk (every {{set: 25 | steps per System 1 chunk}} steps). A flow-matching head (rectified flow, {{set: 10 | flow integration steps}} integration steps) is the expressive alternative.

**How System 1 is supervised.** Actions are normalised with a scaler fitted on the training data, and the Gaussian is factorised over every action number with a learned spread. For a training window, a goal k blocks ahead is drawn uniformly from {{set: 1 | nearest hindsight goal in blocks}} to {{set: 5 | farthest hindsight goal in blocks}}; the target is the {{set: 5 | blocks per System 1 chunk}}-block chunk actually executed, and blocks after the goal frame are masked out of the loss (a masked Gaussian negative log-likelihood), since they do not help reach that goal. Evaluation goals {{set: 25 | goal offset in steps}} steps ahead are exactly {{set: 5 | blocks per System 1 chunk}} blocks, inside this training range; goals {{set: 50 | long-horizon goal offset in steps}} steps ahead are beyond it, which is what the subgoal network is for. An episode counts as a success as soon as the goal condition holds at any step within the budget.

**Two-level System 1.** A subgoal network maps the current and goal latents to an intermediate latent, which System 1 then pursues. A gate fixed before evaluation uses the subgoal only when the squared latent distance to the goal exceeds the median distance covered in one policy horizon on training data, so short tasks are left to System 1 alone.

**Selection and verify-then-act.** For selection, {{set: 16 | candidates per decision}} candidate chunks (the mean plus {{set: 15 | sampled candidates per decision}} samples) are rolled out in the world model, and the one whose imagined end state is closest to the goal latent is executed. Verify-then-act accepts System 1's chunk when its imagined cost falls to {{set: 0.6 | acceptance ratio of verify-then-act}} × the current cost or below one typical block displacement, and otherwise falls back to CEM.

**Memory under camera dropout.** With decisions every block, each frame is lost independently with probability {{set: 0.5 | probability that a camera frame is lost}}. A lost frame is replaced by a blank, by the last frame seen, or by the world model's prediction from the last latent and the actions executed since.

**Units and dependence.** The paired unit is a start/goal pair. The same {{set: 60 | evaluation pairs per seed, GPU study}} pairs are used for every model and seed, so the three seeds are independent training runs evaluated on one shared set of scenarios, not independent evaluation sets. An effect that holds in every seed is robust to training randomness; it does not by itself show that the effect generalises to other scenario sets. A pooled McNemar test treats the outcomes of different seeds (and, where stated, different models) as independent although they share these pairs, so its p-values overstate the evidence; the bootstrap does not. A linear (ridge) probe fitted on training frames and scored on held-out frames gives the read-out errors, reported as mean Euclidean distance in cm.

# 6. Discussion and limitations

In this reduced-scale setting the results point one way: success depends strongly on how well the latent places the task's object, and the world model earns its place as a source of training signals, long-horizon subgoals and memory, not as a per-decision judge.

**What this suggests for practitioners.** Make the fast policy the controller, and test it at the rate at which it will act: a chunk policy that decided every step did much worse than one committing to its chunk, even when trained for single steps. Ground the latent on the state the task is scored on (here the cube; for driving, other agents and free space), even if that state is available only in training. Use hierarchy and model-based filling where horizons are long or sensors drop out. Invest in selection or verification only once the proposals vary in ways that matter and the model can resolve them.

**Limitations.**

- Two simulated environments, one of them near the ceiling (TwoRoom); no physical robot.
- Three training seeds for the main models and one for C6 and C7; the evaluation pairs are shared across seeds, so pooled tests overstate the evidence (Section 4).
- Goals come from later frames of held-out expert episodes, so the results concern reaching expert-like goals, not arbitrary ones.
- The share of pairs already solved at the start was measured on data we generated with the authors' collector, not on their released dataset.
- Camera dropout was simulated as independent frame loss at {{set: 50% | share of camera frames lost}}; longer or correlated outages were not tested.
- The feedback-frequency study (Section 5.8) used the smaller CPU-study models with two seeds; the authors' GC-IDM code ran on our latents and our evaluation, not with the full-scale LeWM encoder it was designed for, so it is not a reproduction of their results.
- Object grounding needs privileged state during training (a simulator, motion capture or a detector).
- The non-trivial filter changes what "success" means relative to published Cube numbers; Appendix A reports the standard protocol for comparison.

**Next.** Section 5.8 leaves open why a single-step System 1 fails where single-step inverse dynamics works; changing its goal distances, loss and amount of training one at a time is the direct next test, and a policy that predicts how long to commit is a natural extension. Since perception of the object appears to limit success, the next model should add resolution where the action happens, for example a second view cropped around the gripper, with the coarse view feeding the subgoal network and the fine view feeding System 1. Its first test is the cube read-out error, then System 1 success and the selection ceiling.
